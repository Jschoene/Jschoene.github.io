Jonas P. Schöne

Lecturer (Assistant Professor) in Social Psychology, University of Southampton

School of Psychology

University of Southampton

Highfield Campus, SO17 1BJ

Emotional expressions are at the heart of nearly every social interaction. Humans have evolved to interpret them quickly and accurately, but many of today’s interactions happen in technology-mediated environments like social media or even with artificial intelligence. Do the psychological mechanisms that once supported emotion sharing still function as intended in these new contexts?

To address this question, I study how people express and perceive emotions across both face-to-face and digital settings, and I work to identify when these processes operate effectively and when they begin to break down. My research spans three lines: what shapes the diffusion of emotional expressions on social media, how people perceive emotional expressions as they unfold over time in both face-to-face interactions and on social media platforms, and the consequences of emotional expression on social media, including ongoing work on human interactions with AI chatbots. To test theory-driven hypotheses, I use a multi-method approach that combines experiments, surveys, AI-powered conversational agents, big data analyses, and field studies.

Before joining Southampton in 2026, I was a Postdoctoral Fellow at Stanford University (with Robb Willer, Jeanne Tsai, and Johannes Eichstaedt). I hold a D.Phil. in Experimental Psychology from the University of Oxford (with Brian Parkinson and Amit Goldenberg at Harvard Business School).

    Filtering negative affective content from social media increases well-being
    Jonas P. Schöne, Tiziano Piccardi, Cindy Wang, and 3 more authors
    2025
    Under Review
    Abs Bib

    A browser extension that tracked participants’ Twitter/X feeds in real time and experimentally reduced exposure to high-arousal negative content. Across two preregistered longitudinal studies (7,455 total observations; N = 309), users reported higher life satisfaction during weeks when negative content was filtered, and filtering also reduced self-reported anxiety and depression.

    Jonas P. Schöne, Shashanka Subrahmanya, Tingting Hsu, and 3 more authors
    2025
    Under Review
    Abs Bib HTML

    Analyzed 1.53 billion geo-located Twitter/X posts from 5.25 million U.S. users, benchmarked against 1.9 million Gallup survey responses. Developed a demographically weighted prediction model to estimate life satisfaction across 1,208 U.S. counties (r = .70), outperforming traditional indicators such as median household income.

selected publications

  1. JPSP
    Overestimation in the aggregation of emotional intensity of social media content
    Jonas P. Schöne, Matthew D. Rocklage, Brian Parkinson, and 1 more author
    Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2025
    Abs DOI Bib HTML

    People systematically overestimate the average emotional intensity of text-based social media responses. Across five studies (N = 1,051), participants consistently overestimated the average emotional intensity, with consequences for how they perceive public opinion and news articles. This overestimation reveals a new cognitive mechanism through which social media contributes to the amplification of emotions.

  3. PNAS Nexus
    Negative Expressions are Shared more on Twitter for Public Figures than for Ordinary Users
    Jonas P. Schöne, David Garcia, Brian Parkinson, and 1 more author
    PNAS Nexus, 2023
    Abs DOI Bib HTML

    Negativity is shared more for public figures than ordinary users on Twitter (N = 6,678 users; 855,715 posts). Among ordinary users, 22% of posts were negative and 24% of shared content was negative. For public figures, 21% of posts were negative but 31% of shared content was negative. This differential effect was fully mediated by follower count and political-content frequency.

  4. NHB
    Amplification in the evaluation of multiple emotional expressions over time
    Amit Goldenberg*, Jonas P. Schöne*, Zhiying Huang, and 7 more authors
    Nature Human Behaviour, 2022
    Abs DOI Bib HTML

    Across nine studies (N = 1,583), participants consistently overestimated the average emotional intensity of sequences of emotional expressions, driven by disproportionate attention to the most intense expressions. This overestimation extended beyond controlled stimuli to videos of people recounting real emotional stories.

  5. AffSci
    Negativity spreads more than positivity on Twitter after both positive and negative political situations
    Jonas P. Schöne, Brian Parkinson, and Amit Goldenberg
    Affective Science, 2021
    Abs DOI Bib HTML

    Negative posts are more likely to be reshared than positive ones across both positive and negative political events on Twitter. Across two studies analyzing over 1.7 million posts, the most widely diffused negative posts often referenced outgroups, hardship, and anger.
